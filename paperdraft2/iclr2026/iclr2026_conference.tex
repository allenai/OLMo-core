% Abstract 2:

% Our pitch: We identify and analyse high-CTC tasks, an underexplored class of LCLM tasks which have very different architectural and scaling properties from more commonly used simpler tasks, and we should study them more.

% The range of such analyses are vast, from well-studied problems like search (e.g. \emph{``Find articles on which year Ralph Lauren was founded''}), to many others (e.g. \emph{``Order these historical documents in the order they likely occurred in'', ``Find all undocumented contradictions in sociolinguistics literature''}), but such tasks are typically studied in isolation without a unified understanding of what's needed to solve them, especially at scale.

Corpus reasoning tasks require extracting and integrating information from large collections of documents (e.g. scientific literature, LLM agent traces), ranging from factoid question answering (e.g. “When was Ralph Lauren founded?”), to underexplored queries like “What conflicting claims appear in this literature?” Different types of tasks often yield different empirical trends, but the reasons remain poorly understood. We introduce a unified definition of corpus task complexity (CTC), which characterizes tasks by how suboperations needed to handle tasks grow with corpus size. Under this definition, we find most existing benchmarks to be \emph{low} CTC with only linear suboperation growth in corpus size, and we identify 10 diverse, understudied tasks belonging to a new class of \emph{high} CTC. Consistent with growing computational demand, we find that on higher CTC tasks, across long context language models, performance degrades more rapidly at larger corpora—even with iid training. These tasks frequently change modeling decisions, and there’s no free lunch: linear-cost chunk-attention methods scale well on low-CTC tasks but struggle on high-CTC tasks, and methods capable of handling high-CTC tasks demand quadratic cost in corpus size. Across diverse findings---from attention to  length generalization---our analysis reveals limitations obscured by solely relying on low-CTC evaluations. We present scalable high CTC reasoning as a new open problem and release our code, eval suite (\bench), and data up to 10M-token scale to support future work.

% Corpus reasoning tasks, which require extracting and integrating information from large collections of documents (e.g. scientific literature, LLM agent traces), range from well-studied tasks such as factoid question answering (e.g. \emph{``When was Ralph Lauren founded?''}), to more complex, underexplored queries like \emph{``What are all the conflicting claims in this literature?''}. We observe that on these ``complex'' tasks, efficient architectures can scale much worse than they do on more well-explored tasks.  We develop a unified view---corpus task complexity (CTC)---to systematically distinguish complex tasks from simpler ones in terms of the smaller operations needed to solve them (and how such operations scale with corpus size): for example, we classify tasks which compare all possible document pairs as ``more complex'' than factoid retrieval. We first investigate new long-context language model (LCLM) training dynamics challenges on these tasks, and propose an effective new mask mixing technique that improves O(N) attention approaches on high \cname tasks, but at larger corpus scale we ultimately find that there is no free lunch: (i) methods that scale efficiently with corpus size \textbf{do poorly when \cname is high}, and (ii) \textbf{methods that \emph{can} handle these tasks scale quadratically in cost with corpus size}. We identify high-\cname tasks as a long-term open problem and encourage more future work investigating new methods to overcome these limitations.
