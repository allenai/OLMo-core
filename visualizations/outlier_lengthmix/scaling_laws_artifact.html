Length-Mix Scaling Laws

How much training data dense and sparse-landmark attention need to reach a given performance at a given context length — measured on three tasks, ~90 training runs, 600-example evals throughout, and reduced to a small set of fitted laws.

How much data and compute, for how much performance

All numbers use the recommended recipe — short-heavy length mix (45/27/16/8/4% of tokens at 2k→32k, geometric tail extended when targeting longer) at each architecture's optimal LR (dense 5e-6, sparse 1e-5). Cell format: training tokens / H200-GPU-hours (GPU-hours from the measured throughput model — dense pays attention on the mix's token-weighted length, sparse pays ~1/64th of it, which is why sparse wins compute at length even where it needs more tokens).

01

Fix the learning rates first

Everything downstream compares architectures, so each got its own LR sweep (outlier, 2k data, 5,000 examples, f1@3k): dense peaks at 5e-6 → 0.781 (two seeds: .781/.752), sparse at 1e-5 → 0.578. Sparse wants ~2× the LR — its landmark pathway is freshly initialized and needs the update budget. All fitted runs below use these optima; the earlier matched-LR (2e-5) copies exist only for the mix-vs-pure control.

02

Pure-length ladders → the Hill law

For each length L, train on N examples of pure length-L data and score at the length-L rung. Every curve fits a saturating Hill form:

f1(N) = fmax · Nγ / (Nγ + Kγ)

K is the data constant (examples to half-max) and γ the steepness. Dense is a gentle γ≈0.6 curve that learns from the first 250 examples; sparse is a sharp γ≈2.7 sigmoid — near-floor until a takeoff threshold, then a rapid climb to (nearly) dense's ceiling. The sigmoid is a protocol-learning signature: landmark writing (compress a chunk) and reading (retrieve from summaries) are useless separately, so no gradual reward exists until both co-emerge.

03

The length exponent is a task property

Refit K at each length. On outlier (find 3 outlier docs among hundreds — cross-document aggregation) the constant explodes: K = 559 → 2,899 → 10,989 examples across 8k → 16k → 32k. Solving each fit for f1 = 0.9 gives the campaign's central law:

N0.9(L) ∝ L1.5  ⇒  tokens0.9(L) ∝ L2.5

On nq, N0.9 grows from ~0.9k to ~10k examples across 8k→64k — β ≈ 1.1 at a task constant ~15× smaller than outlier's. On qdmatch_nq, apparent flatness at short lengths was its lexical shortcut: once bag-of-words stops working (0.55@32k, 0.37@64k), the true pairwise task surfaces with a rising K and a falling ceiling. Refined law: N0.9(L) = Ctask · Lβ with β ≈ 1–1.5 for every real task and C spanning ~15–50× — apparent β≈0 is a saturation or shortcut artifact, and the data bill is always dominated by the highest-C·Lβ task in the mix.

04

Mix shapes, then scale the winner

Four token-budget-matched length distributions were raced (uniform, short-heavy, long-heavy, two-point) at 16–128M tokens. Short-heavy wins or ties for dense and wins decisively for sparse — short data is the fuel for sparse's bootstrap, and dense is nearly shape-indifferent (only two-point fails: without mid-length coverage the middle rungs never form). Scaling short-heavy across five budgets, each arch gets a clean budget law:

Transfer rules learned along the way — ρ(ℓ→L) = ℓ/L additively for dense, but only catalytically: a rung with zero direct mass stays at floor (pure-2k scores ~0.10 at 8k where the additive model predicts 0.87). Transfer is also non-monotonic: on qdmatch, growing 2k data from 2.5k→10k examples collapses the 8k rung from 0.62 to 0.02 (length-overfitting); NQ shows the same shape mildly. Mixes dodge both traps by construction, which is much of why they're the recommended recipe.

No task shows sparse ahead of dense at any rung or any budget. The failure is not one phenomenon: it ranges from a constant ~0.9× factor (outlier) through a step-and-flat penalty (oolong) and compounding decay (nq) to exact chance (reorder, contradiction). Two diagnostics matter when reading these: a metric floor (grouping's pairwise-F1 gives ~.44 for a single-cluster answer, so .419 is chance, not partial credit), and the budget axis — sparse absence looked dead at 20M and only revealed its takeoff at 40M and 80M, so a single small arm cannot distinguish "cannot learn" from "has not started".

Where fit_crossover.py does report a crossing on oolong — 301M at 8k, 460M at 16k — it is 4–6× beyond the largest measured budget and is driven by sparse's fitted ceiling running into its 1.05 bound while dense's bends to .72. That is a statement about which curve has shown its ceiling, not a prediction of crossover, and the tool now labels it as such.

05

Where sparse works at all

taskoperation crossing chunk boundariessparse outcome
nq (single-doc retrieval)find one relevant doc works at 2k, decays with length — at a matched 48M tokens sparse holds 94% of dense at 2k (.912 vs .973) but 28% at 32k (.248 vs .873)
outlier (aggregation)compare all docs to a majority topic works, late — takeoff ~8–16k examples @8k; same ceiling as dense (.967 vs .980 at 64k ex)
qdmatch_nq (pairwise matching)exact M×N query↔doc assignment never — floor at all tested scales; well-formed answers, wrong pairs

Sparse viability is set by whether the required cross-chunk operation survives landmark compression, not by task family — but the length axis matters as much as the operation. Of the three tasks now measured against dense at a matched budget, two (qdmatch, nq) show sparse collapsing as context grows, and only outlier shows the stable constant-factor penalty (≈0.9× of dense, then a plateau) that the "sparse just needs more tokens" story assumes. And on cost: sparse's FLOP advantage (1.16× at 16k, ~28× at 5M, ceiling 64×) is mostly neutralized by the short-heavy recipe — cheap short tokens are cheap for dense too. Sparse's remaining niche is where long tokens are unavoidable (transfer breaks, or the data must be long).
