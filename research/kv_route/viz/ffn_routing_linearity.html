Does Routed FFN Compute Buy Speed?

Re-measured after a bug left twelve of thirty-two FFN layers dense. Model-level arms first, then the kernel-level curves that explain them — every one on the same x-axis, mean FFN compute c. Times are measured medians on one H200; FLOP and byte counts are calculated.

Correction: a third of the FFN was never routed

Every FFN and stacked number this page previously carried was understated. --ffn-start-layer defaulted to 12, so on Qwen3.5-4B (32 blocks) twelve of thirty-two FFNs stayed dense. The arm nominally called “every token takes the null rung” was in fact cutting the FFN only to 0.375 of its own share. Both defaults are now 0 and both benchmark scripts print the routed-layer list at startup (# FFN routed layers (32/32)), so it cannot recur silently. The dense control was re-measured every round; drift 1.004x at 4B and 1.009x at 27B. The kernel-level curves further down are unaffected — they never went through the layer selector.

These are pinned-operating-point costs, not achieved training speedups. Every number on this page is what the hardware does when the token distribution is forced to a given sparsity. A parallel real-training run shows that a learned router under a budget hinge does not reach its target sparsity inside a short (31-step) SFT epoch, so a training job that merely switches these routers on will not realise these numbers. Read them as the wall-clock price of an operating point — an upper bound on what routing could pay if a router got there — and never as a measured training speedup.

Does the FFN axis convert? Yes, and at a flat rate

Measured wall-clock speedup of a whole forward+backward step against the mean FFN compute the router leaves per token, both axes logarithmic. The curve is nearly flat because the FFN is only 26% of a 4B step (46% at 27B): even taking c from 0.20 to 0.02 — a 10× further cut — buys 6%. What the panel really shows is that the conversion rate does not decay along this axis: it sits at 0.65–0.72 (4B) the whole way down. The two rules mark the c=0 arms, which cannot be drawn on a log axis: routing every token to the null rung, and deleting those layers outright.

The gap between the two rules is the whole argument for static deletion: identical FLOPs, 3.6% more speed at 4B and 6.8% more at 27B, because deletion carries no per-layer router prologue, no dispatch histogram and no host synchronisation.

What the router actually costs: kernel launches

Measured launch count for one training step. The routed FFN issues up to 2.13× as many kernels as dense at 4B and 2.28× at 27B, for a fraction of the work. Static deletion sits below dense (6,132 against 6,920 at 4B). That difference — not FLOPs — is what separates the best trainable arm from the best deployment arm once the levers are stacked.

On the full stack this shows up directly: at 4B/16k the best trainable configuration measures 6.46x and the same operating point with the FFN layers deleted instead of routed measures 8.39x. Where that ends, and why GDN rather than the FFN is what stops it, is the subject of the companion page.

Does compute convert to time?

Three token counts, 4B geometry. Where a curve tracks the grey slope-1 guide, FLOPs are converting to time; where it flattens onto a plateau, they are not. The dashed rule is the activation floor — an elementwise pass moving the same activations with no FFN compute at all.

Null rungs, narrow rungs, and a standard MoE

Same mean compute c on every curve — only how the budget is spent differs. narrow-only reaches a low budget by shrinking rungs; full-or-null gives a few tokens the full FFN and the rest nothing; ladder + null keeps small mid rungs; standard MoE splits the same hidden units into E experts and lights top-k of them, which is what every production sparse model does. A narrow rung still reads x and writes a full-width output, so it converges on the plateau; a null token skips all of it. A MoE has a third problem on top of the plateau: each of the k live experts reads the token's d_model vector separately, so its floor is set by k, not by c — which is why top-2 runs 4–5× under top-8 at the same budget once both are past the plateau knee. Only one MoE point anywhere on these four panels gets under the null curve: top-2 at 0.8B, where the whole FFN is 9.4% of a step and the prize is 0.35 ms.

The MoE geometry Qwen actually ships barely cuts per-token FFN compute, and at 4B and below it raises it. The hollow squares are the expert geometry of Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (256×512, 8 routed + 1 shared), -122B-A10B (256×1024) and -397B-A17B (512×1024, 10 routed + 1 shared), dropped into each dense model's d_model. Their live width per token is 4,608 / 9,216 / 11,264 units — against a dense Qwen3.5-4B FFN of 9,216 that is c = 0.50, 1.00 and 1.22, and even against 27B's 17,408 it is only 0.26–0.65. A MoE reads as sparse only against its own total parameter count (c ≈ 0.035 there); against the dense model it replaces it holds per-token FFN compute roughly constant and spends the sparsity on capacity instead. That is a different product from the one this page is buying.

Plotted times are the balanced-capacity bmm — literally MoEMLP.forward in this repo — with a perfectly balanced router and the permutation already done. That is a floor, and it is generous to the MoE: the tooltip's with dispatch figure adds gate, sort, gather, scatter and combine in plain PyTorch and costs a further 4–5× on top of that floor at every scale — enough to put the MoE FFN above the dense one at 0.8B and 4B. Production kernels fuse most of that; none of them removes the k-fold read of x.

What the trained routers actually do

Argmax decisions from two trained Qwen3-4B checkpoints over real contradiction prompts (5.3M token-layer decisions each; rungs 9728/608/152/38/9/1/0). The router learned layer pruning, not per-token routing. Only layer 0 stays dense; 13 of 36 layers (target .05) and 22 of 36 (target .02) switch the FFN off for essentially every token. The 608 rung is selected 0.0% of the time in both. And 52–65% of tokens that do any work sit on width 1 or 9 — rungs costing ~1e-4 of the budget while paying the same fixed activation traffic and kernel launches as any real rung.
